A Dot Product Is
All You Need

Emad Mostaque

A single positive dot product, read under the stated rules, determines the quantum structure generated by a free scalar field: its Hilbert space, evolution, flat relativistic symmetry and Born weights. Two scales remain free: $m$ and $\hbar$.

Read the paper (PDF, 4 pages) Watch the proof See the Lean proof

1The axiom

Axiom. Displacements are measured by a single positive dot product $g$.

Fix a real affine space of dimension $D\ge 2$. The dot product on its translation space determines lengths, orthogonality, reflections and a Laplacian $\Delta_g$. A reading is a number $\Phi(f)=\int f\varphi$: a real scalar field $\varphi$ sampled by a real Schwartz test function $f$. The axiom fixes the geometry, not the readings. A statement about them carries a probability, and the joint distribution of every finite list of readings is the law. Which law does the axiom determine?

What the axiom leaves open is governed by probability: its sum and product rules, symmetry, and maximum entropy. By the axiomatic method nothing else is assumed. For readings these say:

Sum rule
Laws are nonnegative, normalized and countably additive on exclusive (orthogonal) events; a sublist has the marginal law.
Product rule
Independently prepared parts multiply; selecting an event makes it certain and keeps the ratios of its subevents.
Equal values
Equivalent descriptions receive equal values: those related by isometries of $g$, of the covariance form and of the state space.
Maximum entropy
Given the lattice site and edge mean squares that $g$ measures (§2.3), the law maximizes entropy, assuming nothing else.

Theorem 1 (Determination). Under the stated rules, with the lattice moments specified in §2.3, for each pair of scales $m,\hbar>0$ the axiom determines exactly one law of the readings: the centred Gaussian law with covariance $\hbar(-\Delta_g+m^2)^{-1}$. Its readings, paired with their reflections in a plane, generate, uniquely up to a unitary preserving them, a state space with unitary dynamics, tensor composition, Born probabilities and ideal projection conditioning. The resulting theory satisfies the von Neumann and Wightman axioms.

2The law and the operator

2.1Pythagoras

Sum rule Product rule

Squared lengths of orthogonal displacements add. The rules give readings the same geometry. The sum rule makes expectation linear, normalized and nonnegative on squares, so $\langle X,Y\rangle=\E[XY]$ is a positive inner product on centred readings. The product rule makes independent centred readings orthogonal, $\E[XY]=\E[X]\E[Y]=0$, so their squared lengths add:

$$\|X+Y\|^2=\|X\|^2+\|Y\|^2.\tag{1}$$

The rules and the axiom meet in this one equation, and it is read twice: with the product rule it gives the law (§2.2), with the sum rule the Born rule (§3.5).

2.2The law

Product rule Equal values Sum rule

Let $C=\hbar A^{-1}$ be the covariance of the law, $A/\hbar$ its precision. For a finite list of tests $f_i$, linearity $\sum_i\xi_i\Phi(f_i)=\Phi(\sum_i\xi_if_i)$ reduces the joint characteristic function to one reading $\E e^{i\Phi(f)}$ at $f=\sum_i\xi_if_i$. With $u=\xi^{\mathsf T}G\xi$, $G_{ij}=\langle f_i,A^{-1}f_j\rangle$, the reading $\Phi(f)$ has squared length $\hbar u$. Equal values for the isometries of this form make $\E e^{i\Phi(f)}=F(u)$ radial, and the sum rule gives the same $F$ for every list.

Readings of two independent copies, with forms $u$ and $v$, are orthogonal, so their sum has squared length $\hbar(u+v)$. The rotations that mix the copies are isometries too, and the product rule multiplies their characteristic functions. Thus

$$F(u+v)=F(u)F(v),\qquad F(0)=1,\qquad F(u)=e^{-\hbar u/2}.\tag{2}$$

Indeed $F$ is real and $F(u)=F(u/2)^2\ge 0$; continuity excludes zeros, so $\log F$ is real, additive and nonpositive, and the variance $\hbar u$ fixes $F'(0)=-\hbar/2$. Fourier inversion then determines every finite joint law given $A$. Two independent fair signs show the force of this: the first coordinate never vanishes, yet a $45^\circ$ rotation zeros it half the time, failing the isometry test.

2.3Locality and reflection positivity

Maximum entropy Product rule

The law (2) leaves $A$ open. On a finite lattice of $N$ sites with edge-difference matrix $B$ and graph Laplacian $L=B^{\mathsf T}B$ (eigenvalues $\ell_1,\dots,\ell_N$, not all equal), what one dot product measures of $\varphi$ are the site and edge-difference mean squares $\tr C=S>0$ and $\tr(LC)=rS$, with interior ratio $\ell_{\min} $$N(\bar\ell-r)=\beta\sum_{i=1}^N w_i(\ell_i-r)^2.$$

So $\beta>0$ exactly when $r<\bar\ell$, readings smoother than white noise, and the constant mode $\ell_1=0$ forces $\alpha>0$: then $\hbar=1/\beta>0$ and $m^2=\alpha/\beta>0$. In continuum notation the unnormalized lattice density is

$$\exp\!\Big(-\frac{1}{2\hbar}\int\big(|\nabla\varphi|_g^2+m^2\varphi^2\big)\Big).\tag{3}$$

As the mesh goes to zero on growing free boxes, with $m,\hbar$ held fixed, finite-difference consistency and the uniform bound $m^{-2}$ carry the finite joint laws to the centred Gaussian law (2) with $A=-\Delta_g+m^2$: covariance $\hbar(-\Delta_g+m^2)^{-1}$, precision local and of degree one in $-\Delta_g$.

In a free lattice box symmetric about the plane through sites at $s=0$, with reflection $\theta:s\mapsto -s$, fixing the plane field splits the exponent of (3) into a term for each half. By the product rule the two halves are independent given that field, and reflection exchanges them. A real preparation $X$, a function of readings at $s>0$, paired with its reflection therefore has squared length

$$\langle\theta X,X\rangle=\E[\theta X\cdot X]=\E\big[(\E[X\mid\text{plane}])^2\big]\ge 0.\tag{4}$$

For pulses, tests concentrated at $s_i>0$ with strengths $c_i$, this reads $\sum_{i,j}c_ic_jK(s_i+s_j)\ge 0$, where $K(\tau)$ is the covariance of two pulses at normal separation $\tau$.

2.4The operator

Two pulses

Conversely, no local invariant precision of higher degree passes the reflected test (4). Translation invariance makes the coefficients of a local finite-order differential precision constant, and orthogonal invariance makes its Fourier symbol a polynomial $P(|p|_g^2)$, positive with a gap like the precision of §2.3. Fix a transverse momentum $k$, leaving normal momentum $q$, and let $K_k(r)$ be the inverse Fourier transform of $1/P(q^2+|k|^2)$. For the squared operator, $P(x)=(x+m^2)^2$, the normalized kernel is $\kappa(r)=(1+\omega_k|r|)e^{-\omega_k|r|}$ with $\omega_k=\sqrt{|k|^2+m^2}$, and for distinct pulse positions its Gram determinant is

$$\kappa(2r)\kappa(2s)-\kappa(r+s)^2=-\omega_k^2(r-s)^2e^{-2\omega_k(r+s)}.\tag{5}$$

One negative determinant rules out the square. One pair of opposite pulses, strengths $1,-1$ at $r,2r$, rules out every higher degree. With $u=qr$, the expression $\cos 2u-2\cos 3u+\cos 4u=-u^2\cos 3u\,\sinc^2(u/2)$ has modulus at most $u^2$, and for $\deg P\ge 2$ dominated convergence gives

$$\lim_{r\to 0^+}\frac{K_k(2r)-2K_k(3r)+K_k(4r)}{r^2}=-\int_{\R}\frac{q^2}{P(q^2+|k|^2)}\,\frac{\dd q}{2\pi}<0.\tag{6}$$

Smearing narrowly in $s>0$ and transversely keeps the reflected form strictly negative for an actual test function. Degree zero gives no coupling between separated points. Degree one is not an assumption: it is the only nonconstant degree that survives the reflected test. Positivity leaves $P(x)=b(x+m^2)$, with $b$ absorbed in $\hbar$: this is (3). Its kernel $K_k(r+s)=e^{-\omega_k(r+s)}/(2\omega_k)=K_k(r)K_k(s)/K_k(0)$ factors through the plane: given the plane the two sides are uncorrelated, hence independent, giving (4) in the continuum.

3The quantum structure

3.1States

Reflection

The reflected overlaps $\langle[X],[X']\rangle=\langle\theta\bar X,X'\rangle$ of vectors $[X]$, one for each preparation $X$, fix the squared norm of every finite linear combination, so quotienting zero-norm combinations and completing determines the minimal complex state space $\HH$ uniquely up to a label-preserving unitary. By (4), conditioning $[X]\mapsto\E[X\mid\text{plane}]$ embeds $\HH$ isometrically into plane $L^2$: a state is what the plane knows of its preparation. The plane characters are limits of conditioned preparations and are total, so conditioning is onto. In the complexification of the real test space the reflected covariance is an inner product of features $Jf$:

$$(Jf)(k)=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2\omega_k}}\int_0^\infty e^{-s\omega_k}\hat f(s,k)\,\dd s,\qquad C(\theta\bar f,f')=\langle Jf,Jf'\rangle_{\hh},\qquad \hh=L^2(\dd k;\C).\tag{7}$$

The range is dense: a fixed nonnegative bump in $s$ has a strictly positive Laplace transform, so it divides out on each compact momentum set. The Gaussian law now fixes inner products of nonlinear readings as well. For the Wick exponential $W(f)=\exp(i\Phi(f)+C(f,f)/2)$, evaluating (2) at $f'-\theta f$ gives $\langle\theta\overline{W(f)},W(f')\rangle=\exp\langle Jf,Jf'\rangle$. Expanding the exponential gives the inner products of

$$\varepsilon(u)=\bigoplus_{n\ge 0}\frac{u^{\otimes n}}{\sqrt{n!}}\quad\text{in}\quad \HH=\bigoplus_{n\ge 0}\hh^{\otimes_s n}.\tag{8}$$

The tensors are symmetric and the $n!$ is the exponential's: the excitations are identical bosons. By Gram uniqueness, $[W(f)]\mapsto\varepsilon(iJf)$ is the unique label-preserving unitary identifying $\HH$ with Fock space. On any nonzero feature $u$, the states at $0,u,\dots,(n-1)u$ have Gram matrix $(\exp(j\ell\|u\|^2))$, a Vandermonde matrix with nonzero determinant for every $n$, so $\dim\HH=\infty$.

3.2Evolution

Continuation

A shift by $s$ multiplies $Jf$ by $e^{-s\omega_k}$, continuing uniquely into $\RePart z>0$ as $e^{-z\omega_k}$ with unitary imaginary boundary. On the $n$-particle sector phases multiply, giving

$$U(t)=e^{-itH/\hbar},\qquad H_n=\hbar\sum_{j=1}^n\omega_{k_j},\qquad i\hbar\dot\psi=H\psi\ \text{ on }\Dom H.\tag{9}$$

A particle of momentum $p=\hbar k$ has energy $E(p)=\hbar\omega_k$: Einstein's relation $E(p)^2=|p|_g^2+M^2$ is Pythagoras once more, with rest mass $M=\hbar m$, and sums stay in the forward cone. Removing the rest-energy phase and scaling $p=\varepsilon p_0$, $t=\tau/\varepsilon^2$ gives $(E(\varepsilon p_0)-M)/\varepsilon^2\to|p_0|_g^2/2M$: the one-particle evolution converges to the free Schrödinger evolution.

For a real plane test $h$ with $\|h\|_2=1$, the plane reading $Q(h)$ and its time derivative $P(h)=\dot Q(h)$ obey $[Q(h),P(h)]=i\hbar I$: plane readings commute, the shift does not, and the $2\times 2$ Gram determinant gives Heisenberg's bound $\Delta Q\,\Delta P\ge\hbar/2$. Because $H_n\ge n\hbar m$, the spectrum of $H$ lies in $\{0\}\cup[\hbar m,\infty)$, so the vacuum $\varepsilon(0)$, alone at zero, is unique. Energy over $\hbar$ is the rate at which the plane forgets a receding preparation, and $m$ is the slowest such rate.

3.3Special relativity

Continuation

A rotation by $\vartheta$ in a plane containing the normal continues, at $z=s\to it$ and $\vartheta=i\eta$, to a boost of rapidity $\eta$. Boosts preserve $\dd k/2\omega_k$ in (7), so they act unitarily and $t$ is the time of the Poincaré group. The continued field $\Phi(f)=c^*(u_f)+c(u_{\bar f})$, with $u_f$ given by (7) with $e^{it\omega_k}$ in place of $e^{-s\omega_k}$, is an operator-valued tempered distribution with cyclic vacuum.

The heat kernel writes the Euclidean two-point function at separation $(z,x)$ as $G_E(z^2+|x|_g^2)$, holomorphic for $\RePart w>0$. On spacelike sets, $|x|_g>|t|$, both boundary values $z=\varepsilon\pm it$ converge to $G_E(|x|_g^2-t^2)$, so the commutator $[\Phi(f),\Phi(f')]$ vanishes there. The continued form $|x|_g^2-t^2$ has null cone $|x|_g=|t|$: the invariant speed is finite and nonzero, $1$ in units of $g$.

3.4Composition

Product rule

By the product rule the plane laws $\mu_1,\mu_2$ of independent systems multiply, giving $L^2(\mu_1\otimes\mu_2)\simeq\HH_1\otimes\HH_2$; in Fock form $\hh_{12}=\hh_1\oplus\hh_2$ and $\varepsilon(u\oplus v)\mapsto\varepsilon(u)\otimes\varepsilon(v)$ is unitary.

3.5Sharp probabilities

Sum rule Equal values

A sharp event is a projection $P$; the sum rule and equal values govern its weight $p_\psi(P)$ in a unit state $\psi$. By §3.1, $\HH$ is infinite dimensional and separable. By unitary invariance every rank-one event perpendicular to $\psi$ has the same weight; arbitrarily many are mutually orthogonal, so that weight is zero. Countable additivity extends this to their closed spans, and the part of any event's range perpendicular to $P\psi$ has weight zero.

The remaining ray and $\psi$ are classified by their squared overlap $x=\|P\psi\|^2\in[0,1]$, so $p_\psi(P)=f(x)$ with $f(1)=1$. Three orthogonal directions realize every split $x+y\le 1$, so Pythagoras and additivity give $f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y)$. Put $d(x)=f(x)-x$. Complement symmetry gives $d(1-x)=-d(x)$, and doubling gives $2d(x)=d(2x)$ for $x\le\tfrac12$ and $2d(x)=d(2x-1)$ for $x>\tfrac12$, so $2d(x)$ is again a value of $d$. Since $f\le 1$, $M=\sup d<\infty$ satisfies $M\le M/2$, so $d\le 0$, and by symmetry $d=0$:

$$f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y),\qquad f(1)=1,\qquad f(x)=x.\tag{10}$$

Conversely these weights add by Pythagoras. Vector states are rays, normal mixtures are density operators, and projection-valued measures $P$ on $\R$ give self-adjoint observables $A=\int a\,\dd P(a)$ on $\Dom A$.

3.6Ideal conditioning

Product rule

For a density operator $\rho$, write $p(P)=\tr(\rho P)>0$. Certainty of $P$ supports the conditional state on its range. By the product rule its value on each subprojection $Q\le P$ must be $p(Q)/p(P)$. Rank-one subprojections fix all matrix elements by polarization, giving uniquely the Lüders projection update

$$\rho_P=\frac{P\rho P}{\tr(\rho P)},\qquad \psi_P=\frac{P\psi}{\|P\psi\|},\tag{11}$$

with the ray update on the right for a pure state $\psi$ with $P\psi\ne 0$.

4The axioms

Under the stated rules, the resulting free scalar theory satisfies the von Neumann axioms for states and measurement and the Wightman axioms for the field. The Osterwalder–Schrader axioms, the Euclidean form of Wightman's, hold for the continuum law of §2.3; §3.1–3.3 are their reconstruction, here one conditional expectation and one continuation.

5One equation

Four results above are solutions of one functional equation, in the form its rule gives it:

$$\phi(a+b)=\phi(a)\,\phi(b)\quad(\text{product rule}),\qquad \phi(a+b)=\phi(a)+\phi(b)\quad(\text{sum rule}).\tag{12}$$

For a product solution with $0<\phi\le 1$ and $\phi(c)<1$, the function $x\mapsto\log\phi(cx)/\log\phi(c)$ is an additive solution on $[0,1]$ with values in $[0,1]$ and value $1$ at $1$, so the doubling argument of §3.5 solves both.
